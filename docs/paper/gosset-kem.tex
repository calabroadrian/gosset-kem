\begin{abstract}
Replacing the one-bit-per-coefficient message encoding of ML-KEM (FIPS~203) by a multi-dimensional lattice code lowers the decryption failure rate (DFR); the margin can be spent on smaller ciphertexts or on more noise. We study the Gosset lattice $\E$ in this role.
We specify the encoding and decoding, derive the DFR by exact convolution with a union bound over the 240 Voronoi-relevant vectors of $\E$, and validate the underlying independence assumption on $1.28$--$19.2$ million blocks of real ring encryptions.
We define \emph{Compact} parameter sets (6--10\% smaller ciphertexts, unchanged noise) and \emph{MaxSec} parameter sets (FIPS~203 sizes, more noise). For MaxSec, the lattice-estimator reports $+5$ to $+11$ bits on the weakest of the key and ciphertext LWE instances.
Our main contribution is a \emph{correlation-based failure-boosting attack} that applies to any multi-dimensional message code. We prove that the conditional covariance between two decryption-error coefficients equals $\sigma_S^2$ times the negacyclic autocorrelation of attacker-known ciphertext randomness. A code that decodes along directions combining several coefficients is therefore sensitive to ciphertexts that an adversary selects by grinding messages; the standard 1-bit decoder is not.
We give the exact form of this sensitivity, a tail model for it (validated against Monte Carlo within 15\% down to $10^{-4}$), and the resulting attack costs. With consecutive coefficient blocks, the cost of a first failure with at most $2^{64}$ queries drops to $2^{127}$ for the level-1 Compact set, below its claimed security. We prove that a coefficient layout in which the 28 pairwise index distances of each block are distinct reduces the worst-case sensitivity by a factor $2.24$, and we exhibit such a layout. It raises the cost to $2^{192}$ while leaving sizes, speed and the average DFR unchanged. We also prove that no such layout exists for the Leech lattice.
Finally, we report a negative result for $\E$-structured \emph{noise}, a constant-time and a first-order masked implementation, and compiler-induced timing leaks found during testing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

ML-KEM~\cite{fips203} encrypts a 256-bit message by mapping each bit to $0$ or $\lceil q/2\rfloor$ in one coefficient of a polynomial, and decrypts by rounding each coefficient. Lattice codes pack message points more densely in several dimensions, so a larger decryption noise is tolerated for the same failure probability~\cite{saliba21,liu23,paiva24,liu26}. Liu and Sakzad recently proposed codes related to $\E$ for Kyber~\cite{liu26}.

Prior analyses compute the decryption failure rate of such schemes as an \emph{average} over honestly generated ciphertexts. In the IND-CCA setting the adversary chooses which ciphertexts to submit, and failure-boosting attacks~\cite{danvers19,danvers20} exploit ciphertexts whose failure probability is above average. The question we ask is whether the multi-dimensional structure of the code gives the adversary new ways to find such ciphertexts. It does, and the effect is large enough to matter at NIST level~1.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Scheme and correctness} (\S\ref{sec:scheme}--\ref{sec:dfr}). A complete specification of $\E$ message coding inside ML-KEM (Algorithms~\ref{alg:enc}--\ref{alg:dec}), a proof that decoding errors are bounded by a union over the 240 relevant vectors (Lemma~\ref{lem:union}), exact DFRs for all three levels, and an empirical validation of the independence assumption (Table~\ref{tab:valid}).
\item \textbf{Parameter sets and lattice security} (\S\ref{sec:sec}). Compact and MaxSec sets, evaluated with the lattice-estimator~\cite{estimator} on both LWE instances, and a search showing that MaxSec is optimal under FIPS-size and FIPS-DFR constraints.
\item \textbf{Correlation-based failure boosting} (\S\ref{sec:attack}). This is our main contribution. It consists of:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
\item the covariance identity (Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}) and the variance-ratio formula (Lemma~\ref{lem:ratio});
\item the exact sensitivity of the consecutive layout and of distinct-distance layouts (Proposition~\ref{prop:layout});
\item a diagonalisation of the tail problem (Lemma~\ref{lem:eig}) with a Chernoff bound and a saddlepoint estimate, both validated;
\item attack costs, directional boosting after the first failure, and an impossibility result for the Leech lattice (Proposition~\ref{prop:leech}).
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{A negative result} (\S\ref{sec:negative}). $\E$-structured noise loses $\lt D$ bits per block (Proposition~\ref{prop:entropy}), and real lattice-reduction attacks confirm it.
\item \textbf{Implementation} (\S\ref{sec:impl}). Constant-time decoding, a first-order masked decapsulation with a bitsliced masked $\E$ decoder, and timing results on two compilers and two CPU vendors.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What is proven and what is measured.} Lemmas and propositions are proven. Statements about the decryption failure rate at $2^{-140}$ and below, and about attack costs, rely on stated modelling assumptions (independence and Gaussian conditional models) that are standard in this literature~\cite{kyber,danvers19}. We validate each assumption where it is measurable and say so explicitly. Lattice-security figures are produced by the public lattice-estimator. All code and data are public (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\paragraph{Notation.} $q=3329$, $n=256$, $R_q=\Z_q[X]/(X^n+1)$. For $x\in\Z_q$, $\cent(x)$ is its representative in $[-\lfloor q/2\rfloor,\lfloor q/2\rfloor]$. Vectors of polynomials are bold. $\beta_\eta$ is the centred binomial distribution $\sum_{i=1}^{\eta}(a_i-b_i)$ with $a_i,b_i$ uniform bits; it has variance $\eta/2$. $\mathrm{Compress}_d(x)=\lceil (2^d/q)x\rfloor \bmod 2^d$ and $\mathrm{Decompress}_d(y)=\lceil (q/2^d)y\rfloor$.

\paragraph{K-PKE (FIPS~203).} Key generation samples $\mathbf A\in R_q^{k\times k}$, $\mathbf s,\mathbf e\leftarrow\beta_{\eta_1}^k$ and outputs $\mathbf t=\mathbf A\mathbf s+\mathbf e$. Encryption of $m$ with coins derived from $m$ samples $\mathbf r\leftarrow\beta_{\eta_1}^k$, $\mathbf e_1\leftarrow\beta_{\eta_2}^k$, $e_2\leftarrow\beta_{\eta_2}$, and outputs
\[ \mathbf u = \mathrm{Compress}_{d_u}(\mathbf A^T\mathbf r+\mathbf e_1),\qquad v=\mathrm{Compress}_{d_v}(\mathbf t^T\mathbf r+e_2+\mu), \]
where $\mu=\mathrm{Encode}(m)\in R_q$. Decryption computes $w=\mathrm{Decompress}_{d_v}(v)-\mathbf s^T\mathrm{Decompress}_{d_u}(\mathbf u)$ and $m=\mathrm{Decode}(w)$. ML-KEM applies the Fujisaki--Okamoto transform with implicit rejection (FO$^{\not\perp}$): the coins are $G(m\|H(\mathrm{ek}))$, decapsulation re-encrypts and compares, and the IND-CCA2 bound contains a correctness term $\delta$~\cite{hhk17}.

\begin{lemma}[Decryption error]\label{lem:err}
Let $\mathbf c_u=\mathrm{Decompress}_{d_u}(\mathbf u)-(\mathbf A^T\mathbf r+\mathbf e_1)$ and $c_v=\mathrm{Decompress}_{d_v}(v)-(\mathbf t^T\mathbf r+e_2+\mu)$ be the compression errors. Then $w=\mu+\delta$ with
\[ \delta = \mathbf e^T\mathbf r-\mathbf s^T(\mathbf e_1+\mathbf c_u)+e_2+c_v \in R_q. \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$w=\mathbf t^T\mathbf r+e_2+\mu+c_v-\mathbf s^T(\mathbf A^T\mathbf r+\mathbf e_1+\mathbf c_u)$. Since $\mathbf t^T\mathbf r=\mathbf s^T\mathbf A^T\mathbf r+\mathbf e^T\mathbf r$, the terms in $\mathbf A$ cancel.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Errors as inner products.} For $a\in R_q$ with coefficient vector $(a_0,\dots,a_{n-1})$, the product with $b$ has coefficient $i$ equal to $\sum_j a_j\,\rot_i(b)_j$, where $\rot_i(b)_j=b_{i-j}$ if $j\le i$ and $-b_{n+i-j}$ otherwise (negacyclic convolution). Define the secret $S=(\mathbf e,-\mathbf s)\in\Z^{2kn}$, the \emph{ciphertext randomness} $C=(\mathbf r,\mathbf e_1+\mathbf c_u)\in\Z^{2kn}$ and the offset $G=e_2+c_v\in\Z^n$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inner}
\delta_i=\langle S,C_i\rangle+G_i,\qquad C_i:=\rot_i(C)\ \text{(polynomial-wise)}.
\end{equation}
$C$ and $G$ are deterministic functions of $(m,\mathrm{ek})$, hence known to anyone who chooses $m$; $S$ is the secret.

\section{The Gosset lattice as a message code}\label{sec:lattice}

$\D=\{x\in\Z^8:\sum x_i\equiv0\pmod 2\}$ and $\E=\D\cup(\D+\tfrac12\mathbf 1)$, where $\mathbf 1$ is the all-ones vector. $\E$ has determinant 1 and minimum squared norm 2. Its 240 minimal vectors are 112 of type $(\pm1,\pm1,0^6)$ and 128 of type $(\pm\tfrac12)^8$ with an even number of minus signs. They are exactly its Voronoi-relevant vectors~\cite{conway}, and $2\Z^8\subset\D\subset\E$.

\paragraph{Code.} The code is $\mathcal C=\tfrac q2\E\bmod q\Z^8$. Since $q\Z^8=\tfrac q2\cdot2\Z^8\subset\tfrac q2\E$, $\mathcal C$ is well defined, and $|\mathcal C|=[\E:2\Z^8]=2^8$: one byte per 8 coefficients, the rate of ML-KEM. Coset representatives are $x\in\{0,1\}^8$ with $\sum x_i$ even, plus $c\cdot\tfrac12\mathbf 1$ with $c\in\{0,1\}$; this is 128 times 2, so 256 points.

\begin{lemma}[Decoding region and union bound]\label{lem:union}
Let $\hat x$ be a nearest point of the lattice $\Lambda=\tfrac q2\E$ to $p+\varepsilon$, where $p\in\Lambda$ and $\varepsilon\in\R^8$. If $\langle\varepsilon,\tfrac q2u\rangle<\tfrac12\|\tfrac q2u\|^2$ for every relevant vector $u$, then $\hat x=p$. Consequently
\[ \Pr[\hat x\neq p]\ \le\ \sum_{u\in\mathcal V}\Pr\!\Big[\langle\varepsilon,u\rangle\ \ge\ \tfrac q2\Big], \]
where $\mathcal V$ is the set of the 240 minimal vectors. Decoding mod $q$ is decoding in $\Lambda$, because $q\Z^8\subset\Lambda$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The Voronoi cell of $0$ in $\Lambda$ is $\{\varepsilon:\langle\varepsilon,v\rangle\le\frac12\|v\|^2\ \forall v\in\tfrac q2\mathcal V\}$, by the definition of relevant vectors. If $\varepsilon$ lies in its interior, $p$ is the unique nearest point. For $v=\tfrac q2u$ with $\|u\|^2=2$, the condition reads $\langle\varepsilon,u\rangle<\tfrac q2$. The bound follows by the union bound over the complement.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Encoded coefficients are rounded to integers ($832.25\to832$, $1664.5\to1665$, $2496.75\to2497$). This adds a deterministic perturbation of at most $0.75$ per coordinate, which we include in all computations. It is negligible compared with $q/2$.
\end{remark}

\section{Gosset-KEM}\label{sec:scheme}

Gosset-KEM is ML-KEM with three changes: the message code of Algorithms~\ref{alg:enc} and~\ref{alg:dec}, a fixed public coefficient layout $L$ (a permutation of $\{0,\dots,255\}$, Appendix~\ref{app:layout}), and the parameter sets of Table~\ref{tab:params}, which add $\eta=4$ to the centred binomial distribution. Everything else is FIPS~203.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{E8Encode$(m)$, $m\in\{0,1\}^{256}$ as 32 bytes}\label{alg:enc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{$j=0$ to $31$}
  \State $b\gets m[j]$;\ $c\gets b_7$;\ $x_i\gets b_i$ for $i=0..6$;\ $x_7\gets x_0\oplus\cdots\oplus x_6$
  \For{$i=0$ to $7$}
    \State $\mu[L[8j+i]]\gets 1665\,x_i$ if $c=0$, else $832+1665\,x_i$ \Comment{$\mathrm{round}(\tfrac q2(x_i+\tfrac c2))$}
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mu$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{E8Decode$(w)$, $w\in\Z_q^{256}$}\label{alg:dec}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{$j=0$ to $31$}
  \State $Y_i\gets4\cdot\cent(w[L[8j+i]])$ for $i=0..7$;\quad $U\gets2q$
  \For{$o\in\{0,q\}$}
    \State $f_i\gets\lfloor (Y_i-o)/U+\tfrac12\rfloor$;\quad $e_i\gets Y_i-o-Uf_i$
    \If{$\sum f_i$ odd}
      \State $t\gets$ smallest index with $|e_t|=\max_i|e_i|$;\quad $f_t\gets f_t+\mathrm{sign}(e_t)$ \Comment{$\mathrm{sign}(0)=+1$}
    \EndIf
    \State $d_o\gets\sum_i(Y_i-o-Uf_i)^2$;\quad $F_o\gets f$
  \EndFor
  \State $c\gets[d_q<d_0]$;\quad $m[j]\gets c\cdot2^7+\sum_{i<7}(F_{cq,i}\bmod2)\,2^i$
\EndFor
\State \Return $m$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{proposition}[Correctness of decoding]\label{prop:dec}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:dec} returns, for each block, the coset of a nearest point of $\tfrac q2\E$ to $\tfrac14 Y$, with ties broken deterministically. In particular E8Decode(E8Encode$(m)$)$=m$ for every $m$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Scaling by 4 turns $\tfrac q2\E$ into $2q\E=U\E$, which avoids fractions. For each coset $o/U\in\{0,\tfrac12\mathbf 1\}$ of $\D$ in $\E$, lines 4--7 are the Conway--Sloane nearest-point algorithm for $\D$~\cite{conway}. Rounding each coordinate gives the nearest point of $\Z^8$; if its coordinate sum is odd, moving the worst-rounded coordinate one step towards the target gives the nearest point of $\D$. Line 9 keeps the closer of the two candidates, and $\E=\D\cup(\D+\frac12\mathbf 1)$. The message bits are recovered because $f_i\bmod2=x_i$ for the transmitted point; $x_7$ is implied by the parity. For the round trip, the encoded point lies within $0.75$ of a lattice point per coordinate (scaled by 4: $\le3$), far inside the Voronoi cell, whose inradius is $\tfrac q2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot4$.
\end{proof}

We verified Proposition~\ref{prop:dec} exhaustively for all 256 messages, and the constant-time C decoders agree with a reference decoder on $6.4\cdot10^6$ random noisy blocks (0 differences outside exact distance ties).

\section{Decryption failure rate}\label{sec:dfr}

\paragraph{Method.} We compute the exact distribution of a single coefficient $\delta_i$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:err}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item each product $e_jr_\ell$ and $s_j(e_{1,\ell}+c_{u,\ell})$ by enumerating the support;
\item the $kn$-fold sums by repeated convolution;
\item then $e_2+c_v$ by convolution.
\end{itemize}
The compression errors are computed exactly from the rounding maps, assuming uniform inputs, as in the Kyber specification~\cite{kyber}. For the standard decoder this reproduces the published values exactly.

\begin{assumption}[Block independence]\label{ass:ind}
The 8 coefficients of $\delta$ that form a block are treated as independent draws from the marginal distribution.
\end{assumption}

Under Assumption~\ref{ass:ind}, $\Pr[\langle\delta,u\rangle\ge q/2]$ follows by convolving two marginals (type $(\pm1^2)$) or eight marginals scaled by $\frac12$ (type $(\pm\frac12^8)$). The per-message DFR is $32\times$ the block bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:union}. At the ML-KEM parameters this gives $2^{-240.7}$, $2^{-279.4}$ and $2^{-335.0}$, against $2^{-139.1}$, $2^{-165.3}$ and $2^{-175.2}$ for standard decoding. A Gaussian approximation of $\delta_i$ gives $2^{-152.2}$, $2^{-162.7}$ and $2^{-290.9}$, consistent with the $2^{-286}$ reported in~\cite{liu26} for Kyber-1024. It is pessimistic because $c_v$ is bounded and not Gaussian.

\paragraph{Why independence is plausible, and where it fails.} For a \emph{random} ciphertext, $\mathbb E[\delta_a\delta_b]=\sigma_S^2\,\mathbb E_C[\Anc(b-a)]=0$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:cov} below), so the coefficients are uncorrelated on average. They are not independent, since they share $S$. Section~\ref{sec:attack} shows that for \emph{adversarially chosen} ciphertexts the conditional correlations are what matters.

\paragraph{Validation.} We implemented the full ring PKE (any $k$, CBD, compression with $d_u=10$, $d_v=4$) and inflated $\eta$ so that failures become observable. We compared the measured block failure rate with (a) i.i.d.\ coefficients drawn from the exact marginal and (b) the union bound. New keys were drawn for every batch. Table~\ref{tab:valid} shows the results: in the tail ($\eta=12,13$) the union bound matches the measured rate within 0.4\%, and real/i.i.d.\ ratios are consistent with 1.

\section{A negative result: $\E$-structured noise}\label{sec:negative}

Our starting point was to sample the LWE \emph{error} blockwise from the integral copy $L=\sqrt2\E=\{x\in\Z^8:x\bmod2\in H_8\}$, where $H_8$ is the extended $[8,4,4]$ Hamming code and $\det L=16$.

\begin{proposition}[Entropy loss]\label{prop:entropy}
Let $L\subseteq\Z^k$ be a sublattice of index $D$, and let $X$ be supported on $L$. Let $Y$ be the distribution on $\Z^k$ obtained by adding an independent uniform coset representative of $\Z^k/L$ to $X$. Then $H(Y)=H(X)+\lt D$. In particular, at equal per-coordinate variance and in the smooth regime, noise on $L$ has $\lt D$ fewer bits of entropy per block than noise on $\Z^k$ of the same shape.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The map $(x,c)\mapsto x+c$ from $L\times(\Z^k/L)$ to $\Z^k$ is a bijection, because cosets are disjoint and cover $\Z^k$. Hence $H(Y)=H(X)+H(\text{coset})=H(X)+\lt D$.
\end{proof}

For $\E$ this is 4 bits per block, half a bit per coordinate, which we confirmed numerically (3.986--4.000 bits at variances 0.5 to 9).

\paragraph{Attack.} If the error lies in $L^{m/8}$ and $\gcd(D,q)=1$, the primal embedding lattice $\Lambda$ can be intersected with $\{x:\ x_{\text{err}}\in L^{m/8}\}$. The target $(\mathbf e,1)$ is still in the intersection, and its determinant grows by $D^{m/8}$. In the uSVP success condition $\sigma\sqrt\beta\le\delta_\beta^{2\beta-d}\det^{1/d}$, this is equivalent to scaling $\sigma$ by about $D^{-1/8}$, which is $1/\sqrt2$ for $\E$. With the validated estimator, equal-variance $\E$ noise lost 5, 11, 17 and 22 bits at $n=256,512,768,1024$ (equivalent $\sigma$ ratio $0.704$--$0.707$).

\paragraph{Experiment.} We ran a Kannan-embedding attack with LLL ($n=24$, $m=48$, $q=257$, 40 instances per point). At variance 12 per coordinate, a structure-aware attacker succeeded in 60\% of instances on $\E$ noise, the same as on i.i.d.\ noise at half the variance (60\%), and against 0\% on i.i.d.\ noise at the same variance. At variance 8 the figures were 95\%, 92\% and 48\%. The weakness is invisible to an attacker who ignores the structure (30\% at variance 8). Lattice structure belongs in the message, not in the noise.

\section{Correlation-based failure boosting}\label{sec:attack}

\subsection{Attacker model and cost}
We use the model of~\cite{danvers19}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item The attacker knows $\mathrm{ek}$ and chooses messages $m$. Each $m$ determines $(C,G)$ through $G(m\|H(\mathrm{ek}))$.
\item The attacker submits ciphertexts to decapsulation and learns whether decryption failed. This is the standard assumption: a failure yields an unrelated shared key, observable at the protocol level.
\item The attacker precomputes candidates and keeps those in a weak set $\mathcal W$.
\end{itemize}
Let $\alpha=\Pr_m[(C,G)\in\mathcal W]$ and let $\beta$ be the failure probability of a ciphertext from $\mathcal W$ (over $S$). Obtaining one failure takes $Q=1/\beta$ queries and $W=1/(\alpha\beta)$ work. NIST bounds $Q\le2^{64}$. The secret $S$ is fixed but unknown, so we model it as drawn from its distribution.

\subsection{Conditional covariance}
For $C\in\Z^{2kn}$, define the negacyclic autocorrelation $\Anc(d)=\sum_{\text{polys}}\sum_{\ell}C_\ell\,\tilde C_{\ell+d}$, where $\tilde C_{\ell+d}=C_{\ell+d}$ if $\ell+d<n$ and $-C_{\ell+d-n}$ otherwise.

\begin{lemma}[Covariance]\label{lem:cov}
Let the coordinates of $S$ be independent with mean 0 and variance $\sigma_S^2$, and independent of $(C,G)$. Then for $0\le a<b<n$:
\[ \Cov(\delta_a,\delta_b\mid C,G)=\sigma_S^2\,\Anc(b-a),\qquad \Var(\delta_a\mid C,G)=\sigma_S^2\|C\|^2. \]
Moreover $\Anc(n-d)=-\Anc(d)$, so $\Anc(n/2)=0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By~\eqref{eq:inner}, $\Cov(\delta_a,\delta_b\mid C,G)=\sum_j\Var(S_j)(C_a)_j(C_b)_j=\sigma_S^2\langle C_a,C_b\rangle$. Negacyclic rotation is an isometry and $\rot_b=\rot_{b-a}\circ\rot_a$, up to the sign convention. Hence $\langle C_a,C_b\rangle=\langle C,\rot_{b-a}C\rangle=\Anc(b-a)$, which is independent of $a$; for $a=b$ it is $\|C\|^2$. For the symmetry, $\rot_{n-d}=-\rot_{-d}$ and $\langle C,\rot_{-d}C\rangle=\langle\rot_dC,C\rangle$, so $\Anc(n-d)=-\Anc(d)$.
\end{proof}
In ML-KEM, $\mathbf s$ and $\mathbf e$ both follow $\beta_{\eta_1}$, so $\sigma_S^2=\eta_1/2$.

\subsection{Sensitivity of a multi-dimensional decoder}
A block uses positions $p_0<\dots<p_7$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:union}, it fails along a relevant vector $u$ when $\langle\delta_{\text{block}},u\rangle\ge q/2$.

\begin{lemma}[Variance ratio]\label{lem:ratio}
Let $T=\|C\|^2$ and $\rho_d=\Anc(d)/T$. Then
\[ \Var(\langle\delta_{\text{block}},u\rangle\mid C,G)=\sigma_S^2\,T\,\|u\|^2\,R(u),\qquad R(u)=1+\sum_{a<b}\frac{2u_au_b}{\|u\|^2}\,\rho_{p_b-p_a}. \]
For the standard decoder ($u=e_i$), $R=1$ identically.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$\Var(\sum_au_a\delta_{p_a})=\sum_{a,b}u_au_b\Cov(\delta_{p_a},\delta_{p_b})$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:cov} and separate the diagonal terms.
\end{proof}

So for a fixed norm $T$, the failure exponent along $u$ scales by about $1/R(u)$. An attacker who grinds for ciphertexts with large $\max_uR(u)$ boosts failures; the standard decoder is immune. For a random $C$, $\rho_d$ has mean 0 and standard deviation $s_\rho\approx(2kn)^{-1/2}$; we measured $0.022$--$0.035$ across our sets.

\begin{proposition}[Effect of the layout]\label{prop:layout}
For a type-$(\pm\frac12^8)$ vector $u$, write $R(u)-1=\sum_\ell w_\ell\rho_\ell$ over distance classes $\ell\in\{1,\dots,n/2-1\}$, where $w_\ell=\sum_{a<b}\epsilon_{ab}\,\frac{2u_au_b}{\|u\|^2}$, summed over the pairs whose distance $p_b-p_a$ lies in class $\ell$, and $\epsilon_{ab}=+1$ if $p_b-p_a=\ell$, $-1$ if $p_b-p_a=n-\ell$. Each term is $\pm\frac14$. Write $\kappa=\max_u\big(\sum_\ell w_\ell^2\big)^{1/2}$ for the worst-case multiplier of $s_\rho$, assuming the $\rho_\ell$ are uncorrelated.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item With consecutive positions $p_a=8j+a$, $\kappa=\frac14\big(\sum_{d=1}^{7}(8-d)^2\big)^{1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{140}}4\approx2.958$, attained by the all-plus vector. The same distances occur in all 32 blocks, so one ground ciphertext boosts every block.
\item If the 28 distance classes $\min(p_b-p_a,\,n-(p_b-p_a))$ of every block are distinct, then $\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{28}}4\approx1.323$. A given distance appears in at most one pair per block.
\end{enumerate}
For type-$(\pm1^2)$ vectors, $R-1=\pm\rho_d$ for a single distance, so the multiplier is 1 in both layouts.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For type $(\pm\frac12^8)$, $2u_au_b/\|u\|^2=\pm\frac14$. Grouping the pairs by distance class gives $w_\ell$; by Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}, $\rho_{n-\ell}=-\rho_\ell$, so classes merge with a sign. (a) Distance $d$ occurs in $8-d$ pairs, and choosing all signs $+$ makes every contribution add: $w_d=(8-d)/4$. (b) With distinct classes, each $w_\ell$ is a single term $\pm\frac14$ over 28 classes. Uncorrelatedness is supported empirically: the mean correlation between different $\rho_d$ was $-2\cdot10^{-4}$, with maximum $|\,\cdot\,|$ equal to $0.024$.
\end{proof}

Appendix~\ref{app:layout} gives a layout with property (b), found by randomised greedy search. Every distance class is used by at most 9 blocks. Property (b) can be checked directly from the table, and the reference implementation verifies it.

\begin{proposition}[Leech lattice]\label{prop:leech}
For a code based on the Leech lattice $\Lambda_{24}$ with blocks of 24 coefficients, no layout makes all pairwise distance classes of a block distinct.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A block has $\binom{24}{2}=276$ pairs. There are only $n/2=128$ distance classes, and class $n/2$ carries no correlation (Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}). By the pigeonhole principle some class is shared by at least 3 pairs.
\end{proof}
Computing $\kappa$ over the minimal-vector types of $\Lambda_{24}$ ($(\pm4^2)$, octads $(\pm2^8)$, $(\mp3,\pm1^{23})$, scaled to norm 32) gives $4.90$ for consecutive blocks and $2.14$ for the best layout we found, against $1.32$ for $\E$. Higher-dimensional codes are therefore more exposed.

\subsection{Tail of the sensitivity}
The attack cost depends on $\Pr[R(u)\ge1+x]$ deep in the tail. Define the symmetric matrix $Q_u=\sum_\ell w_\ell(P^\ell+P^{-\ell})/2$, applied to each of the $2k$ polynomials, where $P$ is the negacyclic shift. Then $R(u)-1=C^TQ_uC/C^TC$.

\begin{lemma}[Diagonalisation]\label{lem:eig}
$P$ is diagonalised by the vectors $(\omega_j^t)_{t<n}$ with $\omega_j=e^{i\pi(2j+1)/n}$, $j=0,\dots,n-1$. Hence $Q_u$ has eigenvalues $\mu_j=\sum_\ell w_\ell\cos(\pi(2j+1)\ell/n)$ on each polynomial.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
$P^n=-I$, so the eigenvalues of $P$ are the $n$-th roots of $-1$, namely the $\omega_j$, with eigenvectors $(\omega_j^t)_t$ (the negacyclic DFT). $P^\ell+P^{-\ell}$ then has eigenvalue $\omega_j^\ell+\omega_j^{-\ell}=2\cos(\pi(2j+1)\ell/n)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Chernoff bound, Gaussian model]\label{prop:chernoff}
If the polynomials of $C$ have independent $\mathcal N(0,\sigma_p^2)$ coordinates, then for $x>0$:
\[ \Pr\!\big[R(u)-1\ge x\big]\ \le\ \min_{\lambda>0}\ \prod_{p}\prod_{j}\big(1-2\lambda\sigma_p^2(\mu_j-x)\big)^{-1/2}, \]
over the $\lambda$ for which all factors are positive.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The event is $\{C^T(Q_u-xI)C\ge0\}$. Rotate each polynomial into the eigenbasis of Lemma~\ref{lem:eig}; this is orthogonal over $\R$ when the conjugate pairs are combined. The form becomes $\sum_{p,j}\sigma_p^2(\mu_j-x)g_{pj}^2$ with independent standard normals $g_{pj}$. Apply Markov's inequality to $e^{\lambda Y}$ and use $\mathbb E[e^{\lambda a g^2}]=(1-2\lambda a)^{-1/2}$.
\end{proof}

The real entries (CBD plus rounding) are not Gaussian, so we treat Proposition~\ref{prop:chernoff} as a model and validate it. We also use the Lugannani--Rice saddlepoint approximation of the same quadratic form, which is more accurate. Against Monte Carlo with the true entries ($2\cdot10^5$ ciphertexts, Gosset-KEM-512-MaxSec), the results were:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item single direction, type $(\pm1^2)$, at Monte Carlo levels $10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}$: saddlepoint $1.02\cdot10^{-2}$, $0.97\cdot10^{-3}$, $0.97\cdot10^{-4}$; Chernoff $6.8\cdot10^{-2}$, $8.3\cdot10^{-3}$, $9.7\cdot10^{-4}$;
\item single type-$(\pm\frac12^8)$ direction of the distinct layout: saddlepoint $1.01\cdot10^{-2}$, $1.04\cdot10^{-3}$, $1.15\cdot10^{-4}$;
\item union over all directions, $\Pr[\max R\ge r]$: saddlepoint $1.04\cdot10^{-2}$, $0.95\cdot10^{-3}$, $0.79\cdot10^{-4}$.
\end{itemize}
The tails are visibly heavier than Gaussian in $\rho$ (at $3$ and $4$ standard deviations, 2--8 times the Gaussian value). A naive Gaussian model of $\rho$ therefore underestimates the attack, while the Chernoff bound overestimates it.

\subsection{Per-ciphertext failure model and validation}
Given $(C,G)$, we model $\delta_{\text{block}}$ as Gaussian with covariance $\sigma_S^2M$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:cov}) and mean $G$. The failure probability of a block is then bounded by the union over the 240 directions, each with variance from Lemma~\ref{lem:ratio}, averaged over the exact distribution of $\langle G,u\rangle$. We validated this per-ciphertext model on $8\cdot10^4$ real encryptions ($k=2$, $\eta=14$): 299 block failures observed against $303.1$ predicted. Bucketed by $\max_uR(u)$, the counts were 162/147.4 (bottom 50\%), 103/123.2 (50--90\%), 30/28.9 (90--99\%) and 4/3.6 (top 1\%).

\subsection{Attack cost}
The attacker grinds on both $T$ (the norm; $\Pr[T\ge t]$ by a Chernoff bound on the exact distribution of the squares) and $R$ (saddlepoint). We treat the two as independent, since $R$ is scale-free. We optimise $W$ subject to $Q\le2^{64}$, over thresholds $t$ and $x$. With consecutive blocks, one ground ciphertext boosts the chosen direction in all 32 blocks (up to 14 directions per block); with the distinct layout, only the blocks that contain the chosen distance are affected.

\subsection{After the first failure}
Directional failure boosting~\cite{danvers20} uses earlier failures to estimate the direction of $S$. We use its geometric model with attacker-favourable simplifications: the failing direction is known, and each failure contributes a vector with cosine $t=\theta/(\|S\|\|C\|\kappa_c)$ to $S$, where $\theta=q/4$ and $\kappa_c=1$ for the standard decoder, and $\theta=q/2$ and $\kappa_c=\sqrt2$ for $\E$. After $n_f$ failures the estimate has cosine $\rho_{n_f}=\sqrt{n_f}\,t/\sqrt{1+(n_f-1)t^2}$. The attacker grinds for ciphertexts aligned with it, among 256 directions (standard) or $32\cdot240$ directions ($\E$).

The work for the second failure, with $Q\le2^{64}$, is $2^{84.7}$ for ML-KEM-512 against $2^{145.4}$ and $2^{147.9}$ for the 512 Compact and MaxSec sets. At level 768 it is $2^{103.0}$ against $2^{302.2}$ and $2^{200.2}$. Later failures become cheaper in every scheme, but remain costlier for Gosset-KEM than for ML-KEM. The first failure therefore dominates the attack, as for Kyber~\cite{danvers20}. Multi-target attacks reduce the first-failure cost by at most the number of targets; they are not counted, as for ML-KEM.

\section{Implementation and side channels}\label{sec:impl}

\paragraph{Correctness.} The C99/AVX2 implementation passes all NIST ACVP vectors for ML-KEM (75 keyGen, 75 encapsulation, 30 decapsulation, and 60 key checks). An independent reference build, with a bit-level CBD and the Conway--Sloane decoder, agrees bit for bit on all nine sets. A deterministic digest over all sets, the hybrid modes and the DEM is identical with gcc and clang, on Linux and on Windows.

\paragraph{Constant time.} The decoder is branch-free: rounding by comparisons, selections by masks, and value barriers. We used Welch's $t$-test, fixed against random inputs, with percentile cropping and inputs prepared in batches before timing; a positive control decoder contains secret-dependent branches.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Results.} On Intel Xeon and AMD Zen~3 (Ryzen~5 5600X), with gcc~13 and clang~18, all $\E$ decoders gave $|t|\le1.8$ and the reference \texttt{poly\_tomsg} $|t|\le3.6$ at $10^7$ measurements; the positive control reached $|t|>10^4$. Decapsulation of all sets gave $|t|\le3.5$ at $10^6$ measurements, with one exception discussed below.
\item \textbf{Compiler-inserted branches.} clang~18 \texttt{-O3} compiled the mask logic of a portable reference decoder into secret-dependent branches ($|t|\approx1700$); gcc did not. gcc did the same to a Leech decoder. Value barriers fixed both.
\item \textbf{A Zen~3 artefact.} One gcc binary showed $|t|$ of 10--19 for decapsulation, but only when the class was a \emph{repeated} identical ciphertext. The effect was 5--10 cycles out of $85{,}000$, it also appeared for ML-KEM-512, and valid against invalid random ciphertexts remained indistinguishable ($|t|\le1.9$). We attribute it to microarchitectural learning of repeated inputs; it carries no information about $S$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cost.} On Zen~3 with gcc, the $\E$ decoder (AVX2) takes 1{,}554 cycles, against 296 for the reference \texttt{poly\_tomsg}. Decapsulation costs are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item ML-KEM: 82{,}029, 125{,}615 and 181{,}041 cycles at levels 512, 768 and 1024;
\item MaxSec: $+3.7\%$, $+3.0\%$ and $+2.6\%$, mostly from extra SHAKE output;
\item Compact: within $\pm1.2\%$ of ML-KEM.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Masking.} We built a first-order masked decapsulation (Boolean and arithmetic sharing with fresh randomness per gadget):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item Keccak with ISW AND gadgets~\cite{isw};
\item the CBD on Boolean shares followed by B2A conversion mod $q$;
\item an A2B conversion mod $q$ by secure addition and masked conditional subtraction;
\item the ciphertext comparison against public decompression intervals, followed by a masked AND reduction;
\item the key selected on shares, so only the selected key is unmasked.
\end{itemize}
For the decoder we rewrote the $\E$ decision without multiplications: with $a_i=\min(|Y_i|,2q-|Y_i|)$, choose the half-integer coset iff $\sum a_i-4q+p_A(2q-2a_{\max})-2p_Ba_{\min}>0$, where $p_A,p_B$ are the parities of the two candidates. This decision equals Algorithm~\ref{alg:dec} on $4\cdot10^5$ blocks, ties included, and allows a bitsliced masked decoder that processes 32 blocks per word. It takes 34k cycles, 12.3 times faster than per-block masking. Masked decapsulation costs 4.7--6.4 times the unmasked one.

\paragraph{Leakage assessment.} The gadgets are individually first-order probing secure, but we do not give a formal composition proof. Our evidence is a simulated TVLA: Hamming weight of every intermediate share plus Gaussian noise ($\sigma=1$), 43{,}956 points, up to $3\cdot10^4$ traces per class, four runs.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item First order: the maximum $|t|$ was 4.50--5.04. The Bonferroni threshold for 43{,}956 points is 4.87, and the single exceedance did not recur at the same point across independent runs.
\item Second order: $|t|\approx170$, as expected for first-order masking.
\end{itemize}
Hardware validation is open.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
\item Gosset-KEM is not FIPS~203. A compliant deployment can use it only inside a hybrid that includes ML-KEM.
\item DFRs below $10^{-6}$ and the attack costs rest on Assumption~\ref{ass:ind} and on Gaussian conditional models. These were validated where measurable, but the deepest tails are extrapolated, as for ML-KEM.
\item The directional-boosting analysis uses a simplified geometric model.
\item Key generation does not bind the parameter set: $G(d\|k)$ is identical for ML-KEM-512 and Gosset-KEM-512-Compact, so a key pair must never be reused across sets.
\item The masking evidence is simulation only.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
$\E$ message coding buys margin in ML-KEM, either as 6--10\% smaller ciphertexts or as $+5$ to $+11$ bits of lattice security at FIPS sizes. Multi-dimensional decoders, however, are sensitive to correlations between decryption-error coefficients, and those correlations are controlled by attacker-known ciphertext randomness. We characterised this sensitivity exactly. With consecutive blocks it is large enough to break the level-1 claim; a distinct-distance coefficient layout removes most of it at no cost. Higher-dimensional codes such as the Leech lattice are more exposed, and no layout can fully protect them. Any proposal of lattice codes for LWE-based KEMs should include this analysis.
